Three-phase voltage drop: formula and chart

The 1.732 formula, the longest run at 3 % for 208, 240 and 480 V, and a worked example.

Quick answer: three-phase voltage drop is 1.732 × K × I × L ÷ CM, with K ≈ 12.9 for copper. It is 0.866 of the single-phase drop for the same wire, current and length. Example: 40 A on 8 AWG copper over 250 ft at 480 V drops 13.6 V (2.8 %).

Checked 12 November 2026 by Andrii Zhukovskyi. Computed with K = 12.96 Ω·cmil/ft for copper: resistivity 0.017241 Ω·mm²/m at 20 °C plus 25 % for operating temperature, the resistance the voltage drop calculator on this site uses.

Worked example: 40 A on 8 AWG copper, 480 V three phase, 250 ft

  1. Handbook K-factor (K = 12.9): VD = 1.732 × K × I × L ÷ CM = 1.732 × 12.9 × 40 A × 250 ft ÷ 16,510 cmil = 13.53 V.
  2. The calculator on this site uses K = 12.96 and gives 13.60 V line to line, which is 2.83 % of 480 V.
  3. The same wire, current and length on a single-phase circuit would drop 15.70 V: three phase is 0.866 of that.

Result: 13.60 V, 2.83 %, within 3 %.

Questions

What is the three-phase voltage drop formula?

VD = 1.732 × K × I × L ÷ CM, with K ≈ 12.9 Ω·cmil/ft for copper in handbooks (this site's calculator uses 12.96 for copper and 21.3 for aluminum), I the line current, L the one-way length in feet and CM the conductor area in circular mils. The result is the drop between lines; divide by the line-to-line voltage for percent.

Why 1.732 instead of 2?

In a balanced three-phase circuit the currents in the three conductors cancel in the return path, so the line-to-line drop is √3 times the drop of one conductor, not twice as in a single-phase circuit.

Do I use line-to-line or line-to-neutral voltage?

Line-to-line with the 1.732 formula: 208, 240 or 480 V. For a single-phase load connected line to neutral on a three-phase system, use the single-phase formula with the line-to-neutral voltage.

How far can I run 6 AWG on a 480 V three-phase circuit?

About 259 ft one way at a full 65 A before the drop passes 3 %. At half the load the distance doubles.

Does power factor matter?

Yes, on large conductors and motor loads. The table assumes power factor 1; the voltage drop calculator on this site lets you enter the power factor and adds conductor reactance.
