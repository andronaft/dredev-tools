Brix, Plato and SG chart

Convert degrees Brix or Plato to specific gravity and back, with gravity points and the alcohol a must can reach if it ferments dry.

Quick answer: SG = 1 + °Bx ÷ (258.6 − °Bx ÷ 258.2 × 227.1). 10 °Bx is 1.040, 12 °Bx 1.048, 20 °Bx 1.083 and 24 °Bx 1.101. Going back, 1.050 is 12.4 °P and 1.100 is 23.8 °P. Brix and Plato are the same within a tenth of a degree at brewing gravities.

Worked example: a mead must that reads 24 °Brix

  1. Specific gravity: 1 + 24 ÷ (258.6 − 24 ÷ 258.2 × 227.1) = 1 + 24 ÷ 237.49 = 1.1011.
  2. Gravity points: (1.1011 − 1) × 1000 = 101.
  3. Potential alcohol if it ferments dry to 1.000: (1.1011 − 1.000) × 131.25 = 13.3 % ABV; Hall's formula gives 14.4 %.
  4. Back to degrees with the ASBC polynomial: 24.00 °P, the same within a few hundredths.

Result: 24 °Bx is about 1.101 SG, enough sugar for roughly 13–14 % ABV if the yeast takes it dry.

Questions

How do I convert Brix to specific gravity?

SG = 1 + °Bx ÷ (258.6 − °Bx ÷ 258.2 × 227.1). 12 °Bx is 1.0484. For the other direction homebrewers use the ASBC polynomial: °P = −616.868 + 1111.14 SG − 630.272 SG² + 135.997 SG³, so 1.050 is 12.39 °P.

Are Brix and Plato the same?

Both are sucrose percent by weight, measured to slightly different reference tables. At brewing and winemaking gravities they differ only in the third decimal of SG, so homebrew tools, the Ferment app included, use one formula for both.

Is there a simple rule of thumb?

SG ≈ 1 + °Bx × 4 ÷ 1000 is close at low gravities (10 °Bx → 1.040, the formula gives 1.040) but drifts higher up (25 °Bx → 1.100, the formula gives 1.106). Use the chart for musts and strong worts.

Can I use a refractometer reading with this chart?

Before fermentation, yes, after dividing the Brix reading by your wort correction factor (often about 1.04). Once there is alcohol a refractometer reads too high, and the reading needs a separate correction (Sean Terrill's formula, used in the Ferment app), not this chart.

How much alcohol will my must make?

The potential ABV column assumes every bit of sugar ferments down to 1.000. 20 °Bx gives about 10.9 %. Yeast stops early at high gravities, so above about 30 °Bx the number is a ceiling, not a forecast. Measure the final gravity and use the ABV calculator.

At what temperature is SG measured?

These are apparent specific gravities of sugar solutions at 20 °C, what a hydrometer calibrated at 20 °C reads in a pure sugar solution. Read your hydrometer at its calibration temperature or correct the reading.
