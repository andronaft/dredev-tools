Voltage Drop Calculator

Voltage drop in volts and percent, the voltage at the load and the smallest wire that stays under 3 %. NEC (AWG) or IEC (mm²), copper or aluminum.

Quick answer: voltage drop is 2 × K × I × L ÷ CM for single phase (K = 12.9 for copper). The NEC recommends at most 3 % on a branch circuit and 5 % feeder plus branch. Example: 20 A on 12 AWG copper over 100 ft at 120 V drops about 7.9 V (6.6 %), so a long run like that needs 8 AWG.

Worked example: 20 A on 12 AWG copper, 120 V, 100 ft

  1. K-factor method: VD = 2 × K × I × L ÷ CM = 2 × 12.9 × 20 A × 100 ft ÷ 6,530 cmil = 7.90 V.
  2. The calculator's resistance method gives 7.94 V, which is 6.6 % of 120 V. Over the 3 % recommendation.
  3. Going up in size: 8 AWG gives 2.62 %, the first size under 3 %.

Result: use 8 AWG copper for a 20 A load at 100 ft on 120 V, or move the load closer.

Questions

How far can I run 12 AWG wire on 20 amps?

About 45 ft one way at 120 V and 91 ft at 240 V before the drop passes 3 % at full load. At a 10 A load the distance doubles.

How much voltage drop on 100 ft of 10 AWG at 30 amps?

About 7.5 V: 6.2 % at 120 V or 3.1 % at 240 V (copper, one-way length).

What is the voltage drop formula?

For copper under the NEC, VD = 2 × K × I × L / CM for single phase and 1.732 × K × I × L / CM for three phase, with K ≈ 12.9 Ω·cmil/ft, I the load current, L the one-way length in feet and CM the conductor area in circular mils. This calculator uses the equivalent IEC 60364-5-52 Annex G method with conductor reactance, which matches the K-factor result within about 0.5 % at unity power factor.

What voltage drop is acceptable?

The NEC informational notes recommend no more than 3 % on a branch circuit and 5 % for feeder plus branch combined. IEC 60364-5-52 suggests 3 % for lighting and 5 % for other uses on installations supplied from the public network.

Is the length one way or round trip?

Enter the one-way length from the panel to the load. The formula already counts both conductors (or the three phases).

Why does power factor matter?

On AC circuits the drop depends on resistance and reactance. With motor loads (power factor below 1) the reactive part grows, which matters most on large conductors.
