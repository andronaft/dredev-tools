12 V DC voltage drop and wire size chart

The smallest copper wire that keeps a 12 V DC run within 3 % or 10 % voltage drop, for 5 to 50 A over 5 to 50 ft.

Quick answer: at 12 V, 3 % is only 0.36 V. A 20 A load 15 ft from the battery needs 6 AWG copper to stay within 3 %, or 12 AWG if a 10 % drop is acceptable.

Worked example: 20 A load, 15 ft from the battery, 12 V

  1. On 14 AWG: 2 × 15 ft of wire carry 20 A and drop 1.89 V, which is 15.8 % of 12 V.
  2. To stay within 3 % (0.36 V) the wire has to be 6 AWG: 2.47 %.
  3. If 10 % (1.2 V) is acceptable for the load, 12 AWG is enough: 9.93 %.

Result: 6 AWG for 3 %, 12 AWG for 10 %.

Questions

Why does voltage drop matter more at 12 V?

The volts lost depend on current and wire, not on the system voltage. Losing 0.5 V is 0.4 % of 120 V but over 4 % of 12 V, so low-voltage DC runs need much heavier wire for the same percent.

What voltage drop is acceptable on 12 V?

It depends on the load, so this page gives two limits: 3 % (0.36 V) and 10 % (1.2 V). Use the limit the maker of the equipment specifies.

Is the length one way or round trip?

The tables use the one-way distance from the source to the load and already count both the positive and the negative conductor.

What size wire for 20 amps at 12 volts?

For a 15 ft run, 6 AWG keeps the drop within 3 % and 12 AWG within 10 %. Longer runs need larger wire: see the tables.

Does this cover fuse and ampacity?

No. These tables check voltage drop only. The wire must also be rated for the current, and the fuse or breaker must protect the wire. Vehicles and boats have their own wiring standards; follow the one that applies.

How is this calculated?

VD = 2 × K × I × L ÷ CM with K = 12.96 Ω·cmil/ft for copper: resistivity 0.017241 Ω·mm²/m at 20 °C plus 25 % for operating temperature, the resistance the voltage drop calculator on this site uses, set to DC. Cooler wire has slightly less resistance, so real drops are a little lower.
