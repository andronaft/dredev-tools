DC and solar wire size calculator

The wire for a battery, inverter or panel run: enter watts or amps, the one-way length and the voltage drop you allow. AWG, copper or aluminum, with the power lost in the wire.

Quick answer: a 1,000 W load on a 12 V battery draws 83 A; 10 ft from the battery it needs 2 AWG copper to stay within 3 %. The same 1,000 W at 48 V draws 21 A and needs 12 AWG. Higher voltage means less current, thinner wire and less loss.

Questions

What size wire do I need for a 12 V inverter?

It depends on the watts and the distance. A 1,000 W load draws 83 A at 12 V. Voltage drop alone would allow 2 AWG over 10 ft, but the current needs 4 AWG, so the calculator shows 2 AWG. Follow the cable size in the inverter's manual if it asks for more.

Which voltage do I enter for a solar panel run?

The voltage the run works at. For panels in series, add up their working voltages (Vmp) and enter the string's current (Imp). For the battery side, enter the battery voltage.

Why does a higher voltage need thinner wire?

The same power at twice the voltage is half the current. Half the current loses half the volts in the same wire, and those volts are a smaller share of a higher voltage. The table under the calculator shows it for your own numbers.

What voltage drop should I allow?

There is no single rule. The NEC's informational notes recommend no more than 3 % on a branch circuit and 5 % in total. If the manual of your inverter or charge controller asks for less, use that: the calculator offers 1, 2, 3 and 5 %.

Is the length one way or round trip?

One way, from the source to the load. The formula already counts both the positive and the negative conductor.

Does it check ampacity and the fuse?

It compares the current with NEC Table 310.16 at 75 °C (30 °C ambient, no more than three current-carrying conductors) and never shows a size below that, or below 14 AWG. It does not size the fuse or breaker, apply derating or the rules for PV circuits. Vehicles and boats have their own wiring standards; follow the one that applies.

Why does another calculator give a slightly different number?

Mostly the resistance it assumes. This one uses K = 12.96 Ω·cmil/ft for copper: resistivity 0.017241 Ω·mm²/m at 20 °C plus 25 % for operating temperature, the resistance the voltage drop calculator on this site uses, which is under 1 % above the rounded table values. DC has no power factor: a calculator that keeps a power factor below 1 in DC mode shows a smaller drop than the wire really has.
